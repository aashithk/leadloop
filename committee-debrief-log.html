Committee Debrief Log

After an interview loop, interviewers compare notes in a debrief. This page teaches the four generic patterns those conversations revolve around — and gives you a private log to capture your own learnings after every loop.

Educational only. These are generic, widely discussed interview-preparation concepts. They are not observations from any real committee, not quotes from any interviewer, and not statistics about any hiring process.

The four debrief patterns

Each pattern has a free overview. The detailed breakdown unlocks with email.

1. Scope

Debriefs ask: what was the denominator of this person's impact — one feature, one team, or many teams?

Scope is the most common leveling signal in behavioral debriefs. The underlying question is simple: did the candidate's work change what happened for a team, for several teams, or for an organization? Candidates who describe work in feature-sized units get read as feature-sized contributors, whatever their title says. The fix is mechanical: for every story you plan to tell, write down the denominator explicitly — how many engineers, teams, users, or systems were affected — before you walk in. If the denominator is small, pair the story with a larger one.

2. Ambiguity

Debriefs ask: did this person define the problem, or just execute someone else's spec?

Ambiguity separates levels more cleanly than almost any other signal. Junior and mid-level bars can be cleared with excellent execution of well-defined work; senior and staff bars require evidence of finding the problem worth solving. In debriefs this shows up as a question about who wrote the spec. Prepare at least one story where the requirements did not exist when you started — where you talked to stakeholders, narrowed the problem, and defined what "done" meant. Name the moment you chose the problem, not just the moment you solved it.

3. Influence

Debriefs ask: did this person move people who didn't report to them?

Influence without authority is the staff-level currency, and debriefs test for it directly. The pattern interviewers look for is a decision that changed direction where the candidate had no formal power to compel it — a design review that redirected another team, a standard that got adopted voluntarily, a disagreement resolved without escalation. When you tell these stories, make the "without authority" part explicit rather than hoping it lands. Say who owned the decision, what you did to shape it, and what changed because of it.

4. Leveling vs bar

Debriefs separate two questions: "could they do the job?" (the bar) and "at what level have they done it?" (leveling).

This distinction confuses more candidates than any other part of hiring. Clearing the bar means you can do the work; leveling decides which title and compensation band the offer carries. They are decided with different evidence: the bar from any strong signal, leveling from sustained scope, ambiguity, and influence patterns. Practical consequence: a candidate can pass every interview and still be leveled below the target role if the evidence clusters at a smaller scope. If you are targeting a specific level, every story you tell should be pitched at that level's denominator, not just at "good enough to pass."
