XFN Conflict Resolver

Cross-functional conflict is where staff engineers earn the title. Pick the scenario closest to yours — you'll get an incentives read, the staff-level move, and a message you can send today.

Scenario: a partner team is blocking your launch

What's really happening

Blockers are rarely malice — usually the partner team is optimizing for their own launch, their own on-call load, or a constraint you can't see. The conflict is an information problem wearing a people-problem costume.

Their incentives

  • Hit their own commitments without absorbing your risk.
  • Avoid on-call pain and review burden from your change.
  • Be seen as a reliable partner, not a bottleneck — they want this resolved too.

The staff-level move

  1. Map their constraint. Before proposing anything, ask one genuine question: "What's the hardest part of this ask from your side?" Write down what you learn.
  2. Write the options. A one-pager with 2–3 options, each with cost to both sides. People negotiate with documents, not vibes.
  3. Pre-wire. Talk to the partner tech lead 1:1 before any group meeting. No one likes being surprised in front of their team.
  4. Log the decision. Whatever you agree on goes in writing with names and dates. Memory is where agreements go to die.
Say it like this: "I think we're optimizing for different constraints, and I don't fully understand yours yet. Can I get 20 minutes to understand the hard parts on your side? Then I'll write up options with costs for both teams and we can pick together."
Junior handling: escalating to your manager on day one, or building a workaround in secret that becomes a second system to maintain.

Scenario: a PM is pushing a deadline you can't meet

What's really happening

A date is a proxy for a business need. The PM usually doesn't need your date — they need a commitment they can plan around. The conflict is date-vs-scope, but it's being argued as effort-vs-laziness.

Their incentives

  • A date they can communicate upward without looking wrong later.
  • Maximum scope by that date, obviously.
  • Avoid being the person who "slipped."

The staff-level move

  1. Surface the trade-off, don't argue the date. "We can hit June 1 with scope A, or July 1 with scope A+B. Which matters more?" Make the cost visible.
  2. Recommend, don't just list. Staff engineers have a point of view. Name the option you'd pick and why.
  3. Put the choice in writing. When scope is chosen, document who chose it. This protects both of you.
  4. Never silently slip. The moment the date is at risk, say so — early bad news is a gift, late bad news is a betrayal.
Say it like this: "I want to give you a date you can bet on. With the current scope, June 1 is 50/50; with the reporting cut, it's 90%. My recommendation is we cut reporting and commit to June 1. Your call — what does the business need most?"
Junior handling: saying "that's impossible" with no alternatives, or saying yes and then slipping silently.

Scenario: two teams claim ownership of the same problem

What's really happening

Ownership fights are ambiguity fights. Nobody wants to own the on-call; everybody wants to own the roadmap. The longer it's unresolved, the work either duplicates or dies in the gap.

Their incentives

  • Own the visible, promotable work; avoid the thankless maintenance.
  • Not be on the hook for a system they can't staff.
  • End the ambiguity — ownership fights are exhausting for everyone.

The staff-level move

  1. Draft the charter yourself. Don't wait for managers to sort it out. Write a half-page: what the problem is, who owns what, who decides, who gets paged.
  2. Make both leads react. A draft is easier to argue with than a blank page. Send it to both leads: "Here's my proposal — what would you change?"
  3. Split build vs. run if needed. Ownership doesn't have to be one team. Often one team builds and another runs — name both explicitly.
  4. Get the decision blessed. Once the leads agree, get it acknowledged one level up so it survives the next reorg.
Say it like this: "This keeps falling between our teams, so I drafted a half-page ownership proposal — who builds, who runs, who decides. I sent it to both leads for reactions. Happy to adjust; I just want it written down before the next incident decides for us."
Junior handling: doing the work quietly while resenting the other team, or waiting months for management to clarify.

Scenario: your design was rejected in review

What's really happening

A rejected design is feedback with a sharp edge. The reviewers may be right, partially right, or optimizing for something you don't value — but defending in the moment almost never works. Design reviews are won in writing, before the meeting.

Their incentives

  • Protect the system's long-term health (their name is on it too).
  • Avoid setting a precedent they'll regret.
  • Be heard — sometimes "rejected" means "I don't feel understood."

The staff-level move

  1. Steel-man the objection. Restate their concern better than they did. "If I understand you, the worry is X under load Y." This disarms and clarifies at once.
  2. Separate the person from the idea. Thank the reviewer for the catch — publicly. Your reputation compounds from how you lose, not just how you win.
  3. Iterate in writing. Address the objection in the doc, not in a hallway debate. Docs are where decisions actually get made.
  4. Timebox the loop. "I'll revise by Thursday; if there are no new objections, I'll proceed." Reviews that never end are a process failure, not a design failure.
Say it like this: "That's a fair concern — let me restate it to make sure I get it: under 10x traffic, my approach adds a hotspot on the metadata store. I'll add a section to the doc comparing that against the alternatives with numbers, and revise by Thursday."
Junior handling: arguing in the meeting, or quietly resenting the reviewers and disengaging from the design.

Scenario: a dependency keeps slipping

What's really happening

A slipping dependency is an implicit contract failing in slow motion. Nobody agreed to the date, the scope, or what "done" means — so nobody feels accountable when it moves.

Their incentives

  • Their own roadmap comes first; your dependency is interrupt work.
  • Avoid committing to a date they might miss.
  • Minimize context-switching — your "quick ask" is never quick.

The staff-level move

  1. Make the contract explicit. What exactly do you need, by when, and what does "done" look like? Vague asks get vague dates.
  2. Agree on it in writing. A two-line email: "Confirming you'll deliver X by Y, and I'll handle Z." This is the whole game.
  3. Build the plan B early. If the dependency is on your critical path, have a fallback before you need it — not after.
  4. Escalate with options, early. "X is at risk; here are two paths forward" beats "X slipped" every time. Escalation is a tool, not a failure.
Say it like this: "I realize we've never written down what I need from your team and by when — that's on me. Here's a two-line summary: the API shape, the date, and what I'll do on my side. Can you confirm or adjust? I want us both protected."
Junior handling: pinging "any update?" every week, then being surprised when the date moves.
