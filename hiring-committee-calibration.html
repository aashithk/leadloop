How hiring committees level L5 vs L6 vs L7

Same question, three answers — and what a committee hears in each. This preview teaches you to read your own stories the way a calibration room does: scope, ambiguity, influence.

Education only. Every response below is a fictional, illustrative example written for teaching — not a real interview, not a real committee, not real feedback. Leveling varies by company and loop.

The calibration lens: what committees actually weigh

Committees don't score your answers; they compare patterns of evidence across the whole loop. Four patterns separate levels:

1. Scope of impact

The denominator matters: your team, several teams, or the whole org. Higher levels require evidence that your work landed beyond your own backlog.

2. Ambiguity navigated

Who defined the problem? Executing a clear spec is different from discovering the problem, framing it, and getting others to agree it mattered.

3. Influence without authority

Committees look for decisions you drove in rooms where you had no formal power — partners, skip-levels, adjacent teams that didn't report to you.

4. Leveling vs. the bar

Two separate questions: leveling (what scope has this person operated at?) and the bar (will they succeed at the target level here?). A great L6 answer can still be a "no" for L7 if the scope pattern isn't there — and that's not a failure, it's a calibration.

One question, three answers

The prompt: "Tell me about a time you disagreed with a partner team and how it was resolved."

L5-typical pattern

Illustrative fictional example A

"The payments team wanted to change the webhook contract in a way that would have broken our retry logic. I documented the risk, showed them our error rates, and escalated to my manager. We agreed on a two-week migration window, and my team shipped the adapter in time."
Committee lens: Scope = own team. The resolution path runs through the manager (escalation, not influence). Evidence is solid craft — adapter shipped on time — but the denominator is one team's delivery. Reads as strong within-scope execution: consistent L5 signal.
L6-typical pattern

Illustrative fictional example B

"The payments team wanted to change the webhook contract, which would have broken retry behavior for three consuming teams. I pulled the failure data across all three, wrote a one-page proposal comparing options, and met with their tech lead to understand their timeline pressure. We landed on a versioned contract with a deprecation window — they got their launch, nobody broke."
Committee lens: Scope = multiple teams. Data-driven proposal, partner incentives understood, shared outcome. The candidate owned the resolution without authority over the partner team. This is the L6 shape: cross-team problem, self-directed resolution, durable result.
L7-typical pattern

Illustrative fictional example C

"The webhook contract fight kept recurring — every quarter, some team changed a contract and broke someone else. I reframed it: this wasn't a payments problem, it was a missing org standard. I drafted a versioning policy, got the three staff engineers across the org to pressure-test it, and presented it at the eng all-hands. Within two quarters, every public contract had a deprecation window, and this class of incident disappeared."
Committee lens: Scope = the org. The candidate didn't just resolve the conflict — they redefined it as a strategy gap and changed how the organization works. Influence without authority across staff-level peers, a standard that outlived the moment, and a before/after that shows judgment about what matters at scale. This is the L7 shape: the person bends the trajectory of the group, not just the outcome of the project.

Side-by-side leveling table

Scope denominator

L5: own team · L6: multiple teams · L7: org or business-unit direction.

Who defined the problem?

L5: executed a defined problem well · L6: framed an ambiguous problem across teams · L7: named a problem the org hadn't seen and changed the system that produced it.

Resolution mechanism

L5: escalation + execution · L6: influence through data and relationships · L7: standards, processes, or strategy others adopt.

Durability of the result

L5: the project shipped · L6: the conflict resolved for all parties · L7: the class of problem stopped recurring.

How to use this on yourself

  • For each of your best stories, write down the denominator: who besides your team felt the result?
  • Underline the sentence where you defined the problem — if there isn't one, that's your gap.
  • Ask a peer: "Did I influence anyone who didn't report to me?" Collect the names.
  • If everything resolves via escalation, practice one influence path before the loop.
